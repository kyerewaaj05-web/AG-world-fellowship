The Church

The worldwide Assemblies of God is Pentecostal movement, a Christian fellowship committed to proclaiming the Gospel of Jesus Christ, building believers in the faith, empowering people through the work of the Holy Spirit, and establishing churches that serve their communities. The story of AG is one of Christian mission, evangelism, discipleship, sacrifice, leadership development and the growth of a church movement from its beginnings in Hot Springs,1914. The ministry developed within a Pentecostal tradition that emphasized salvation through Jesus Christ, the ministry and gifts of the Holy Spirit, prayer, Scripture, evangelism and the establishment of Christian communities.From its early beginnings, the work of Assemblies of God was not limited to preaching alone. The early missionaries encountered communities facing challenges involving education, literacy, health, poverty and other social needs. Consequently, the development of the church included activities intended to address both spiritual and practical needs. Historical studies of AG describe its early approach as a form of holistic mission, combining evangelism and church planting with Over the decades, the movement expanded beyond its original setting and developed congregations, leaders, ministries and structures across the world. Today, AG describes its mission work in terms of evangelism, church planting, discipleship, leadership development, community development and reaching communities beyond borders.At the heart of AG is the conviction that the Christian faith is not merely something to be discussed but something to be lived. The church seeks to create communities in which people can worship God, hear the message of Scripture, grow in their relationship with Christ, discover and use their God-given gifts, receive spiritual guidance, and participate in the work of serving others. Its ministries extend to children, young people, adults, families, leaders and communities, with evangelism and discipleship remaining central to its identity. Through generations of pastors, missionaries, evangelists, teachers, church leaders and ordinary members, AG has continued to develop as a Pentecostal church seeking to proclaim Christ and participate in God's mission around the world.

1. Origin - Azusa 1906

The story of the Assemblies of God can be traced to the great Pentecostal awakening that spread across the world at the beginning of the twentieth century. One of the most important events in this history was the Azusa Street Revival of 1906, which took place in Los Angeles, California, under the leadership of William J. Seymour. The revival began in the spring of 1906 after a period of prayer, Bible study and spiritual expectation among believers in Los Angeles. William J. Seymour, an African American Holiness preacher who had been influenced by the teaching of Charles Parham, came to Los Angeles and began preaching about the baptism of the Holy Spirit and the spiritual gifts described in the New Testament. After meetings first took place in private homes, the growing group eventually moved to a former African Methodist Episcopal church building at 312 Azusa Street. The meetings at Azusa Street became an extraordinary center of prayer, worship, preaching and testimony. People from different racial, social and denominational backgrounds gathered together. African Americans, whites, Latinos and people from other backgrounds worshipped together at a time when racial segregation was deeply established in American society. Men and women also participated actively in the ministry and prayer meetings. The revival therefore became known not only for its emphasis on the Holy Spirit and spiritual gifts but also for its remarkable interracial character. The revival emphasized the experience commonly described by Pentecostals as baptism in the Holy Spirit, accompanied by spiritual gifts and, particularly, speaking in tongues. Participants understood these experiences in light of the accounts of Pentecost and the spiritual gifts found in the New Testament. The Assemblies of God later developed its own theological formulation around these experiences, including the teaching that speaking in tongues is the initial physical evidence of Spirit baptism. The influence of Azusa Street extended far beyond Los Angeles. Visitors came from different parts of the United States and from other countries. Many returned to their own communities carrying the Pentecostal message and beginning or strengthening ministries. Publications such as The Apostolic Faith also helped spread reports of the revival internationally. The first issue appeared in September 1906, and thousands of copies were eventually distributed. The significance of Azusa Street was therefore not simply that a particular congregation experienced a revival. It became a major point of connection for a rapidly developing Pentecostal movement. People who visited the meetings carried what they had experienced and learned into other cities, nations and churches. Assemblies of God historical materials describe the Azusa Street Revival as one of the focal points of the emerging Pentecostal movement and identify it as a major factor in bringing Pentecostalism before a worldwide audience. The revival continued with remarkable intensity for several years, generally dated from 1906 to 1909. During those years, Azusa Street became an important center from which Pentecostal missionaries, ministers and ordinary believers went out to proclaim the Gospel and encourage believers to seek the work of the Holy Spirit. However, the origins of Pentecostalism were broader than Azusa Street alone. Other revivals, prayer movements and Pentecostal developments were occurring in different parts of the world. For example, the Assemblies of God recognizes the importance of other spiritual awakenings and Pentecostal pioneers in the development of the wider movement. Azusa Street should therefore be understood as a major catalyst and international launching point, rather than the sole source of every Pentecostal development.

2. Founding in 1914

The rapid growth of Pentecostal Christianity after the Azusa Street Revival created new opportunities as well as new challenges. Pentecostal believers were establishing churches, missions and ministries in many places, but there was a growing recognition that cooperation, organization and accountability were necessary if the movement was to serve its expanding mission effectively. By the early 1910s, Pentecostal ministers and churches were increasingly communicating with one another. Many shared similar beliefs concerning salvation, sanctification, divine healing, Spirit baptism, spiritual gifts and the authority of Scripture. They also shared a strong desire to evangelize and send missionaries into places where the Gospel had not yet been widely proclaimed. The question was how these independent ministers and congregations could cooperate while maintaining their Pentecostal convictions and local church identity. This led to the historic gathering at Hot Springs, Arkansas, in April 1914. Approximately 300 Pentecostal ministers and leaders gathered for what became the founding convention of the Assemblies of God. The gathering was not simply intended to create another denomination. The participants were seeking a practical way for Pentecostal believers and churches to cooperate in evangelism, missions, publishing, education and ministry. The official Assemblies of God history identifies this gathering as the formation of the General Council of the Assemblies of God. The participants came together with a strong sense of spiritual purpose. Prayer and seeking God were central to the gathering. The emerging fellowship was intended to provide cooperation without unnecessarily eliminating the independence of local congregations and ministers. One of the important outcomes of the meeting was the formation of a cooperative fellowship that could support ministers and churches while providing structures for missions, education, publications and other forms of ministry. The name "Assemblies of God" became associated with this new fellowship. T. K. Leonard played an important role in the early organization, including writing the constitutional preamble that established the name of the new fellowship. The newly formed organization initially operated with limited resources. Its first headquarters and printing operation were established in Findlay, Ohio, using facilities provided by T. K. Leonard. The headquarters later moved to St. Louis, Missouri, and eventually to Springfield, Missouri, which became the long-term home of the Assemblies of God national office. The founding of the Assemblies of God was therefore a significant organizational development within the wider Pentecostal movement. It transformed a network of ministers and churches into a cooperative fellowship with a clearer structure for carrying out its shared mission.

3.History

The Pentecostal movement placed particular emphasis on the experience and work of the Holy Spirit, prayer, spiritual gifts, evangelism and the expectation that the Gospel should reach people and communities across geographical and cultural boundaries. Assemblies of God developed within this wider Pentecostal environment and eventually became an international fellowship of churches and missions.One of the earliest challenges missionaries faced was communication. The missionaries therefore devoted significant attention to learning the local languages and developing ways of communicating the Christian message to the people.Their work involved evangelism teaching, literacy and practical service, while they also had to adapt to unfamiliar environments and languages. The early mission gradually extended. Historical accounts record the establishment of work in several places during the 1930s. As the number of believers and congregations increased, the church developed structures for leadership, fellowship and administration.Across the decades, the church continued to grow through evangelism, church planting, leadership training, discipleship, missions and community engagement. AG's history therefore is not simply a record of buildings and institutions; it is also a story of missionaries and Christians working together, communities receiving the Gospel, local leaders being trained, churches being established and generations of believers continuing the work. The present identity of AG has been shaped by these many decades of ministry and by the continuing responsibility to proclaim Christ and serve communities.


4. Our Beliefs

The beliefs of the Assemblies of God are rooted in the Christian Scriptures and in the Pentecostal understanding of the work of the Holy Spirit and this influenced The Sixteen Fundamental Truths. Assemblies of God churches affirm the Bible as the inspired Word of God and recognize one true God who is revealed as Father, Son and Holy Spirit. The church teaches that Jesus Christ is truly divine and truly human, that humanity was created good but fell into sin, and that salvation and restored fellowship with God are made possible through Jesus Christ. These beliefs form part of the Assemblies of God's fundamental doctrinal framework and provide the theological foundation for its preaching, worship, discipleship and ministry.A central part of Assemblies of God belief is the reality of salvation through Jesus Christ. The church teaches that people are separated from God by sin but can experience forgiveness, reconciliation and new life through faith in Christ. Christian life is therefore understood not simply as membership in a religious organization but a personal relationship with God expressed through faith, repentance, obedience and a transformed life. The church also emphasizes sanctification: the continuing work of God in the life of a believer as that person grows in holiness and increasingly reflects the character of Christ. Assemblies of God teaching describes sanctification as beginning with salvation while also continuing throughout the believer's earthly life.Another distinctive emphasis is the baptism in the Holy Spirit. Assemblies of God teaching regards this as a special experience in the life of the believer and teaches that speaking in tongues is the initial physical evidence associated with Spirit baptism. The church also places importance on spiritual gifts and the empowerment of believers for Christian witness and ministry. This Pentecostal emphasis has historically influenced the church's approach to worship, prayer, evangelism and missions. Rather than viewing ministry as the responsibility of only pastors or ordained leaders, the Pentecostal tradition encourages believers to recognize that God equips His people to participate in the work of the church and the proclamation of the Gospel.The church also recognizes water baptism and the Lord's Supper as important Christian ordinances. Water baptism is practiced by immersion following salvation, while Communion, or the Lord's Supper, is observed as a remembrance of the death of Jesus Christ. Together with Scripture, prayer, worship, evangelism, discipleship and Christian fellowship, these practices contribute to the spiritual life of the congregation. The Assemblies of God therefore seeks to combine sound biblical teaching with practical Christian living, encouraging believers not only to know what they believe but also to express those beliefs through their daily lives, relationships, service and witness.
